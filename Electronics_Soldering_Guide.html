0. Conventions — read first

  • Solder alloy in stock: Qualitek Delta RA300, Sn60/Pb40, rosin-activated flux core (IPC-J-STD-004 ROM1). Melting range 183–188 °C — not the Sn63/Pb37 eutectic at a single 183 °C point. The 5 °C pasty range means joints can be disturbed during cool-down — hold parts steady ~2 s after the iron pulls away before disturbing. Common spot to miss: tweezer-placed SMD parts; release the tweezers only after the joint goes visibly matte → solid.
  • Working tip temperatures:
    • Through-hole + JST-XH PCB-mount headers: 350 °C with leaded RA300
    • SMD lift + reflow on 0805/0603: 350 °C with fine chisel or fine conical
    • LED MCPCB pads (aluminum substrate): 380–400 °C (the aluminum sinks heat — need the thermal reserve)
    • 20 AWG power buses on perfboard rails: 380 °C for thermal-mass joints
  • Flux: Chip Quik CQ4LF no-clean liquid flux in 10 ml pen applicator. Single tiny dot per joint — the pen tip dispenses precisely, no syringe mess. Wipe SMD work with 99 % IPA after for long-term reliability in humid environments (power bay). RA flux is mildly activated; residues are non-corrosive + non-conductive but cheap insurance to clean.
  • ESD: PCA9685, TMP102, INA226 are CMOS but ESD-tolerant. Mat + wrist strap = cheap insurance, not strictly required.
  • Eye protection: always. The solder spits occasionally, especially when reflowing oxidized joints.
  • One-bridge-per-row safety (INA226 address pads): re-stated from EXTERNAL_SENSOR_INTEGRATION_PLAN.md §2 because it's easy to forget under the iron — never have two bridges in the same address row simultaneously — it shorts VCC to GND through the row trace.
  • Always run a continuity check before bridging anything. Silk letters are unreliable across vendor lots (see the GVLA/GULA correction recorded in the EXTERNAL doc). Function ID by multimeter against known rails on the header is deterministic and takes 30 seconds.

3. Tip Care — the routine that makes everything else easier

This is the section most hobbyists skip and then wonder why their tips die in 6 months. The discipline below is what makes a tip last 5+ years.

3.1 Brass cleaner balls — in-session oxide removal

What it does: strips oxide + old flux + cooked solder off the working face of the tip during a soldering session. The brass wool is softer (Mohs 3) than the tip's nickel-iron plating (Mohs ~5–6) so it scrubs clean without abrading the protective coat.

How to use: with the iron hot and on the stand, stab the working face into the brass balls and twist briefly (~1 second). Oxide + crud falls off. Re-tin (touch a small dab of solder to the tip) immediately to keep the face protected. Repeat every few joints during a long session.

Why not a wet sponge: wet cellulose works but thermally shocks the tip (350 °C → wet surface → instant ~100 °C drop, then back up). Brass balls don't cool the tip — better thermal stability and faster recovery between joints.

3.2 Tip tinner (Thermaltronics TMT-TC-2) — start, stop, recovery

Tinner is a flux + low-temp solder compound that chemically etches oxide AND deposits fresh solder simultaneously. It serves two distinct purposes:

A. Recovery from oxidation (mid-session). If the tip goes dull, grayish, dark, or stops accepting solder:

  1. Iron at working temp (350 °C).
  2. Press the hot tip directly into the tinner paste for 1–2 seconds. Brief hiss + faint smoke = flux activating.
  3. Tip comes out coated in fresh shiny solder.
  4. Wipe excess on the brass cleaner balls.
  5. Tip is reactivated — back to wetting joints in 2 seconds.

B. Preventive tinning (the high-leverage habit). This is what extends tip life from months → years:

  • End of every soldering session: heat the iron, dip the tip in tinner, leave a fat solder coat on the working face. Then turn off the iron. The solder coat protects the plating from oxidation during cool-down + storage.
  • Start of every session: heat the iron, wipe the old solder coat off on the brass balls, dip in tinner again, wipe excess. Fresh-tinned working face in ~30 seconds.
  • Mid-session if the tip starts looking dull: brass-ball wipe + tinner dip + brass-ball wipe again.

The 20 g tin lasts essentially forever at hobby scale — one tin = years of use.

3.3 ⚠️ DO NOT use sandpaper / emery on iron tips

Even fine 600+ grit is alumina (Mohs 9) — harder than the tip's thin nickel-iron plating (~50–100 µm). Sanding tears through the plating in seconds, exposing the copper core which then oxidizes black at 350 °C and stops accepting solder permanently (de-tinning). The tip is unrecoverable from this point — you've destroyed the protective coat that makes it wettable.

This applies whether the tip is hot or cool. Emery is harder than the plating either way.

The bench sandpaper (100–1000 grit, in stock) stays useful for: deburring brass standoffs, abrading bonding surfaces before MG 8329TCM epoxy (see Electronics_Assembly_Guide.md §15), general prep. Just keep it away from iron tips.

3.4 Solder before storage

Final habit: never store an iron with a bare tip. A bare tip oxidizes overnight; next session, it won't take solder until you re-tin. A fat-solder-coated tip stays mint for months. 5 seconds of solder dab before turn-off = 30 seconds saved next session + years added to the tip's lifespan.

4. Technique Reference

Each per-part section in §5+ references one or more of these technique primitives. Read this section once; then for each per-part procedure you only need to know which technique applies.

4.1 Through-hole header soldering

Used for: JST-XH 2.54 mm PCB-mount headers into bare 0.1″ rows on TMP102 boards (§5, §6), 0.1″ pin headers on the rail PCBs (§9), any header into a PTH (plated-through-hole) board.

  • Tip: fine or medium chisel (~1 mm working face)
  • Temp: 350 °C
  • Solder gauge: 0.5 mm
  • Procedure per pin:
    1. Insert the header from the back side so the connector body sits below the PCB.
    2. Apply tip to the joint (touch both the pad and the pin simultaneously) for ~1 s to bring the joint up to temp.
    3. Feed solder onto the joint, not the iron. Wait for it to flow around the pin and form a smooth fillet (conical, shiny, fills the hole).
    4. Remove solder, then remove iron. Total ~3 s per joint.
    5. Inspect under the 10× magnifier — smooth fillet, no bridges to adjacent pads.

Bad signs: dull/grainy joint (cold — reflow), excessive solder (too much wire fed — feed less), solder didn't flow into the hole (joint wasn't hot enough — re-apply iron + a bit more flux).

4.1a Desoldering an existing solder bridge between two pads (address-pad rework)

Used for: removing a factory solder bridge between two adjacent address-select pads — most commonly the INA226 A1:G factory bridge that has to come off before A1:U can be added (§5.1 sets the target address to 0x44 which needs A1=VCC, not the factory A1=GND). Also any similar rework where a small blob of tinned solder bridges two closely-spaced pads and needs to come off cleanly without lifting either pad.

Why this warrants its own section: the operator's first instinct — SS-03 solder sucker — often doesn't work on thin factory bridges. The sucker's nozzle can't get close enough to the flat blob to build vacuum. Braid + fresh flux + fresh solder is the reliable path. (Field-reported difficulty during INA226 A1:G removal — added 2026-08-09.)

  • Tip: fine conical (the finer the better — you want to concentrate heat on the bridge, not on adjacent pads)
  • Temp: 340–350 °C (same as SMD lift — hot enough to reflow, low enough to not lift copper)
  • Solder gauge: 0.5 mm (only used to "re-wet" the old bridge)
  • Flux: Chip Quik CQ4LF essential — a bare cold factory bridge doesn't wick well; flux revives it
  • Desoldering braid: fine-gauge wick (1.5-2 mm width), the type in bench stock (see §1.2)
  • Procedure:
    1. Fresh flux drop on the bridge. Small dot of CQ4LF right on top of the blob and covering both pads. Flux is what makes the difference — a dry, oxidized factory bridge stubbornly resists the braid; a fluxed bridge wicks in one pass.
    2. Optional but recommended: re-wet the bridge with a tiny amount of fresh 0.5 mm solder (~0.5 mm of wire fed). Counter-intuitive — you're ADDING solder to remove solder — but the fresh solder is well-tinned and gives the braid something to grab. Old factory bridges can be alloy-mismatched or oxidized enough that they wick reluctantly without the fresh-solder reset.
    3. Cut ~15 mm of clean desoldering braid and lay it directly on top of the bridge (braid touches both pads + the blob between them).
    4. Press the iron tip down onto the braid so heat conducts through the braid → solder blob melts → capillary action wicks the molten solder INTO the braid. Watch the braid change color (silvery uptake) — that's when it's working.
    5. Wait 2-4 s while the braid absorbs the blob. Do NOT lift the iron off the braid while the solder is still molten — you'll trap the braid to the pad.
    6. Lift the iron and braid together in one motion, while everything is still molten. Braid comes off cleanly. Cut the used section of braid off (it now has old solder in it) — fresh braid for any next pass.
    7. Inspect with the 10× magnifier: the two pads should be flat and DULL (matte grey — the sign of a clean, no-solder pad). Shiny = solder still there. Continuity meter across the two pads should read OPEN circuit.
    8. If not fully clean: repeat steps 1-6 with a fresh braid section + more flux. Some factory bridges need 2-3 passes. Don't rush and don't hold the braid on the pad for > 5 s continuous (copper-lift risk).
  • Cleanup: wipe the area with 99 % IPA to remove RA flux residue. Inspect adjacent pads — no accidental splash-bridges from the wick operation.
  • Gotchas:
    • Do NOT use the SS-03 solder sucker for thin factory bridges — nozzle can't seat, vacuum won't grab a flat blob. Reserve the SS-03 for full through-hole joints (where the solder is a chunky mass), use braid for flat bridges.
    • Too much heat + too long dwell = lifted copper pad. Same failure as SMD lift. Cap the braid dwell at ~5 s per pass; step back and let the board cool between passes.
    • Adjacent-pad splash risk — if the fresh solder in step 2 overshoots, you can accidentally bridge to a NEIGHBOURING pad. Feed the tiniest amount possible (< 0.5 mm of wire). Better to re-add if too little than to bridge the wrong pair.
    • Verify with continuity, not just visual. A blob can look gone under the magnifier and still have a whisker connecting the pads. Beep-mode multimeter across the two pads confirms.

4.2 SMD lift — iron + tweezers ("tin and slide")

Used for: INA226 R4/R5 "01C" pull-up disable (§5.2), PT4115 1R0 sense-R removal (§8.1).

  • Tip: fine conical or fine chisel
  • Temp: 350 °C
  • Flux: drop of Chip Quik CQ4LF on the part
  • Procedure:
    1. Drop of flux paste on the part.
    2. Tin the iron tip lightly.
    3. Add fresh solder to ONE end of the SMD to build up a fat solder blob — feed 0.5 mm solder wire onto ONE termination of the 2-terminal part (0805 has 2 metal end-caps) until you have a chunky puddle that engulfs both the SMD's end-cap AND the PCB pad beneath it. You are adding MORE solder, not less — counter-intuitive but this is the key to a clean lift. The chunky puddle gives the joint enough thermal mass that heat conducts quickly through the whole SMD body when you dwell the iron on it. Small factory joints don't have enough mass — you'd end up dwelling for 20+ s and cooking the pad. (Note: the "both pads on that side" wording in older versions of this doc was confusing — the 0805 has only ONE pad per side. The intent is "a fat blob that covers the SMD's termination end-cap AND its PCB pad in one big lump.")
    4. Grip the part mid-body with fine tweezers (curved tip preferred — won't slip).
    5. Slide the iron across both terminations alternately. Both joints soften progressively as heat spreads through the part body.
    6. Lift straight up (no twist) when both ends are molten. Part comes free with no force. Typical total dwell: 8–15 s for 0805.
  • Cleanup: SS-03 sucker on each pad while heating with the iron, then desoldering braid for residual. Wipe area with 99 % IPA to remove RA flux residue.
  • Inspection: Drdefi 10× magnifier — pads intact, no lifted copper, no shorts to neighbors.

4.3 SMD place + reflow ("tack and flow") — HISTORICAL (no active caller)

HISTORICAL — DO NOT USE IN CURRENT BUILD. This technique's only caller was the retired §8.2 PT4115 0R15 placement. As of 2026-06-18 no driver variant requires an SMD sense-R swap (all three are used at factory current). Kept as a reference for any future SMD-placement task; if you're a new builder, skip this section.

Used for: PT4115 0R15 sense-R placement (§8.2). Reverse of the lift technique.

  • Tip: fine chisel
  • Temp: 350 °C
  • Procedure:
    1. Drop of flux on the pads.
    2. Tweezer-place the new SMD across both pads, centered.
    3. Tack one end with the iron + a small dab of solder — this holds the part in position.
    4. Check alignment — should sit centered. If skewed, reflow + nudge while molten.
    5. Reflow the other end with iron + dab.
    6. Hold tweezers steady ~2 s after the iron pulls away. Sn60/Pb40 RA300 has a pasty range 183–188 °C (not the Sn63/Pb37 eutectic single-point at 183 °C) — joint passes through ~5 °C of mushy phase during cool-down where part movement weakens the joint. Don't release until the joint goes visibly matte → solid.
  • Inspection: under the magnifier — solder fillets on both pads, no tombstoning (one end lifted), no shorts.

4.4 Solder-bridge for address pads (one-blob technique)

Used for: INA226 A0/A1 address-pad bridges (§5.1) — sets I²C address 0x44.

  • Tip: fine conical (the tip's edge, not the flat)
  • Temp: 350 °C
  • Solder gauge: 0.5 mm
  • Procedure:
    1. Flux dot on the chosen pad.
    2. Tin the iron tip lightly.
    3. Single small solder blob between the two halves of the pad. Use the tip's edge to draw the blob across the gap.
    4. The blob should bridge both pad halves cleanly; no spread to adjacent pads (the pads are small, 0.5–1 mm — don't be greedy with solder).
    5. ⚠️ One bridge per row only. Confirm visually that no other pad in the same row is bridged. If one is (e.g., the INA226 factory A1:G bridge that needs to come off before adding A1:U for 0x44), desolder it first per §4.1a "Desoldering an existing solder bridge" — braid + flux, NOT the SS-03 sucker (the sucker's nozzle can't seat on a flat factory bridge; braid + fresh flux + a wetting drop of solder is the reliable path).

4.5 Trace cut + bridge (TMP102 ADD0-style relocation)

Used for: TMP102 wand address mod 0x48 → 0x49 (§7).

This is two discrete operations: a scalpel cut (to break the GND tie) + a solder bridge (to make a V+ tie). Continuity-verify between them.

Cut:

  1. Sharp scalpel or X-Acto #11 blade (fresh — copper dulls blades quickly).
  2. Score the GND-tie trace with 3–5 light strokes until copper is visibly severed under the magnifier.
  3. Continuity-verify: multimeter at the IC's ADDR pin (or the center pad) → known GND on the header → should read OPEN (no beep). If it still beeps, re-score.

Bridge:

  1. Fine iron at 350 °C, fine conical tip.
  2. Flow a small solder blob bridging the ADDR center pad to the V+ rail-tie pad of the address-select cluster.
  3. Continuity-verify: ADDR pin → V+ (header pin) → should beep. ADDR pin → GND → should NOT beep.

4.6 Wire-to-pad on LED MCPCB (high thermal mass)

Used for: 12-channel × 2-wire LED bundle to 3× LED stars (§10).

LED MCPCB substrate is aluminum — large thermal mass that sucks heat out of the iron tip. Requires:

  • Tip: medium chisel (the wider footprint helps transfer heat)
  • Temp: 380–400 °C (leaded RA300; lead-free would need higher)
  • Preheat (optional but recommended): warm the MCPCB on a hot plate at ~100 °C for 30 s before soldering. Reduces iron dwell time on each pad.
  • Procedure per pad:
    1. Tin the pad first (small blob, ~1 s with flux paste).
    2. Place the tinned wire-end on the pad.
    3. Reflow both together (~2 s).
  • Inspection: smooth joint with a small fillet. No cold gray dull joint (those are weak). If you see one, reflow.

4.7 Perfboard bus — BARE stripped solid wire (technique changed 2026-09-05)

RETIRED TECHNIQUE — nothing in this build calls it any more (2026-09-24). Its only caller was §9.3, the bare-wire perfboard rail, and all three rails are now BusBoard ST1 stripboard where the copper strip is the bus. What replaced this section is §4.10 track cuts: you remove copper instead of adding wire. Kept for reading boards already built.

Used for: nothing — no live caller.

✅ No more nicking. The buses are FULLY STRIPPED wire now.

The v9.11 layout removes every crossing, and with it the reason to nick — one net per strip, so nothing ever has to cross. Each bus is a straight run inside one row, and within its span every hole belongs either to that bus or to nothing at all — there is no third net for a bare wire to touch. So use fully stripped solid-core wire (breadboard jumper stock), lay it flat in its row, and solder it into every hole it passes.

  • Wire: fully stripped solid core, ~22 AWG (standard breadboard jumper wire, ~0.6 mm — drops straight into a 1.0 mm perfboard hole). Solid core is what makes this work: it stays where you put it and will not fray a whisker into the next row.
  • Tip: medium chisel. Temp: 380 °C for 13.8 V / GND, 350 °C for the three signal buses.
  • Procedure:
    1. Cut to the span in the bus table, plus a couple of mm.
    2. Lay it flat along its row on the OUTER (wall-facing) face, both ends landing in a hole — never leave a bare end floating over the board.
    3. Solder every hole it passes, not just the connector landings. Unused holes in the span are isolated pads, so a joint there costs nothing and buys strain relief. ~2–3 s per joint with flux.
    4. Continuity-test end to end before laying the next bus.
  • Inspect: every joint shiny; the wire sitting flat with no lift between holes; no strand or burr reaching into an adjacent row.

4.8 JST-XH PCB-mount connector soldering (orientation matters)

Used for: NOT the wand rails — v9.11 has no JST-XH on them at all; every rail landing is 0.1″ berg. The only JST-XH left on the wand is the 6-pin in the END PLATE that the bay tether plugs into (§9.5). Still used for: the two +Y joint boards, which take a 6-pin B6B-XH-A / XHP-6 pair (§9.5).

The orientation choice — body OUT vs body IN through the perfboard — affects how the connector mates with cables vs. external hardware. Read §9 for the per-connector orientation table.

  • Tip: fine chisel
  • Temp: 350 °C
  • Solder gauge: 0.5 mm
  • Procedure: like through-hole headers (§4.1) but with the orientation decision per-connector. Insert connector, solder all pins through the 0.1″ pitch holes from the opposite face.
  • Inspect: smooth fillets, no solder bridges between adjacent pins.

4.10 Stripboard track cut — the technique that replaced §4.7

Used for: the P− ground-bus board (one cut) — and, as of v9.11, that is the ONLY board in the build with a track cut on it.

The wand rails do not use this technique. They run one net per strip and have no cuts — §9.4. The warning below still matters for the ground-bus board's single cut, which is the wand's star point and the highest-stakes cut left in the build.

⚠️ A bad cut is invisible and a good cut looks like nothing

This is the failure mode that makes track cuts different from every other operation in this guide. A cold solder joint looks wrong. A track cut that did not quite sever the strip looks exactly like one that did — same scraped copper, same gap to the eye — and the board powers up and works. On the P− ground-bus board the strip-3 hole-3 cut is the flashlight SKU's entire star ground: if it did not take, P−power and P−logic are one bus, 2.4 A of LED return flows through the I²C reference, and nothing anywhere is visibly wrong until the bus glitches under load. Meter every cut. There is no visual substitute.

  • Tool: a stripboard spot-cutter if you have one, otherwise a 3 mm drill bit turned BY HAND in the hole, or a sharp hobby knife. Not a powered drill — it grabs and tears the pad.
  • Where: at the hole, not between holes. The copper is narrowest at the hole's annulus and the pad gives the cutter something to centre on.
  • Procedure:
    1. Cut before any component goes on the board. Copper is easy to reach now and buried later.
    2. Rotate the bit/knife until the pad's copper ring is gone and you can see bare FR4 all the way round the hole. Two or three turns is usually enough.
    3. Brush the swarf off — a loose copper curl lying across the gap reads as continuity and is the most common false failure.
    4. Meter it: one probe each side of the cut, adjacent holes. Must read OPEN. If it beeps, go back in; do not assume the probe slipped.
    5. Re-meter along each surviving segment end to end. A cutter that wandered can sever the strip a hole early, which fails silently in the other direction.
  • Inspect: bare FR4 visible through the hole, no copper whisker bridging the gap, no lifted pad on either neighbouring hole.

Cut schedule for this build:

Board Cuts What each one does
Wand rails ×3 (ST1, 6 strips × 20) ✅ NONE One net per strip, so no strip is ever shared and nothing needs separating. The star ground is structural — GND-logic on strip 4, GND-power on strip 5.
P− ground-bus board (ST1, 6 strips × 9) strip 3, hole 3 Splits P−power (holes 1–2) from P−logic (holes 4–8). E14 straddles it — see §4.11.

⚠️ The rail's M2 holes are not cuts and sever nothing: they sit between strips 3 and 4 at cols 4 and 17, so each Ø2.2 hole trims both strips' edges but cuts neither (meter 3V3 ↔︎ GND-logic OPEN and both strips continuous afterwards). The ground-bus board's four M2 holes are a different case — see below.

4.11 Surface-solder ACROSS a cut — the star point

Used for: E14, the 18 AWG BMS P− feed on the ground-bus board — the single place where the flashlight SKU's two grounds meet. Exactly one wire in the build is soldered this way.

This wire is the star point. Solder it LAST.

E14 sits on the surface, bridging the strip-3 hole-3 cut you just made in §4.10 — it lands on the copper either side, not in a hole. That is the entire design: P−power and P−logic are otherwise separate, and E14 is the one node joining them. Solder it before the rest of the board and every subsequent continuity check reads short, so you lose the ability to prove the cut took.

  • Why surface and not through a hole: stranded 18 AWG is ~1.1–1.3 mm OD and an ST1 hole is 1.02 mm. It was never going to thread through — so this is not a compromise, it is the intended landing.
  • Tip: large chisel. Temp: 380 °C. 18 AWG on a copper strip is a real thermal load; a fine tip will stall and you will cook the FR4 chasing it.
  • Procedure:
    1. Do §4.10 first and meter the cut OPEN. Then do every other joint on the board. E14 is last.
    2. Tin the strip either side of the cut and tin the wire end separately.
    3. Lay the wire along the strip so it covers both segments with the cut roughly centred under it. Reflow one side, check it has not shifted, then the other.
    4. Meter P−power ↔︎ P−logic — it must now read SHORT, and that is the only route between them. Lift a probe onto E14 itself and confirm both sides reach it.
  • Inspect: the wire flat against the board on both sides of the cut, fillets that wet the strip and not just the wire, and no solder run into the cut gap — a bridge there defeats nothing visibly but moves the star point off the wire.

5. Part 1: INA226 — Address Bridges + Pull-up Disable

Goal: set the INA226 to I²C address 0x44 (one bridge per row on the address pads), and disable its onboard 10 kΩ SDA/SCL pull-ups (lift R4/R5). Header solder is not required — the GODIYMODULES INA226 ships with the 4-pin logic header and 4-pos screw terminals pre-installed at the factory (verify on receipt). Full board reference + photos in EXTERNAL_SENSOR_INTEGRATION_PLAN.md §2 "INA226 board reference."

5.1 Address pads — set 0x44 (technique §4.4)

Confirmed silk on this vendor's board (GODIYMODULES clone): address pads in each row read G U L A left to right (G=GND, U=VCC, L=SCL, A=SDA). Row labels: TOP row = A0, BOTTOM row = A1 (operator-verified — the EXTERNAL doc previously had this inverted; caught + fixed 2026-05-23).

Target 0x44 = A1=VS, A0=GND → bridge:

  • Top row: silk "G" pad (A0 → GND) — leftmost pad in the A0 row
  • Bottom row: silk "U" pad (A1 → VCC) — second pad from left in the A1 row

⚠️ One-bridge-per-row safety. Before bridging anything, verify no other pads in those rows are already bridged. Desolder any existing same-row bridge before adding a new one. End state: exactly 2 bridges total, one per row.

Continuity-verify before bridging (the only deterministic step — silk is unreliable):

  1. Power off, disconnected.
  2. Multimeter → continuity (beep) mode.
  3. The bottom 4-pin logic header (silk GND / SCL / SDA / VCC left-to-right) carries the 4 known rails.
  4. For each of the 8 address-bridge pads, beep against each of the 4 header pins. Each pad beeps against exactly ONE rail.
  5. Mark the pad-to-rail mapping. Now you know which pad to bridge regardless of how the silk reads.

Bridge technique: see §4.4 — fine conical tip at 350 °C, single small solder blob between the two halves of the chosen pad. Don't bridge to neighboring pads.

Bench check after: with the INA226 wired to the G469, docker exec ArmPiUltra i2cdetect -y 1 must show 0x44. If 0x40 → no bridges took. If 0x41 → rows mirrored (redo continuity check). If 0x4X for any other X → wrong pad somewhere.

5.1a INA226-BATT — set 0x45 (added 2026-10-23; same board, same technique)

The second INA226 (the battery-lead monitor, Power_Bay §5a) sits at 0x45 = A1 → VS, A0 → VS: top-row "U" (A0 = VCC) + bottom-row "U" (A1 = VCC). Follow §5.1's procedure exactly: continuity-ID every pad against the header rails FIRST (the silk is ambiguous), one bridge per row, never two in the same row, then i2cdetect → 0x45. Reading 0x44 means the top-row bridge went to G; 0x41 means the rows are mirrored. Lift both 01C pull-ups (§5.2) on this board too. Mounting on a carrier build uses the board's dead Ø2.0 @ 17.5 mm pair, never the live Ø2.2 ISENSE± pads (Power_Bay §11.3.9).

5.2 Pull-up disable — lift R4 + R5 (technique §4.2)

Location: the two SMD resistors silk-marked "01C" on the right edge of the board are R4 + R5 = SDA/SCL pull-ups to VCC. EIA-96 decode: 01C = 10 kΩ (see §4.9 decoder table). (The doc previously said "10K" silk — wrong; corrected 2026-05-23.)

Procedure: see §4.2 SMD lift technique. Specifically:

  1. Drop of Chip Quik CQ4LF flux on each resistor.
  2. Tin and slide with the Yanecty (350 °C, fine chisel or conical tip).
  3. Tweezer-lift each resistor straight up when both ends are molten.
  4. Place lifted resistors in a parts tray (label "removed" — don't reuse).
  5. Clean pads with SS-03 sucker, then desoldering braid for residue.
  6. Wipe area with 99 % IPA — RA flux residue cleanup (humidity-prone power bay environment).
  7. Inspect under magnifier: pads intact, no lifted copper.

Verify: with both resistors lifted, multimeter SDA-pad → VCC pad → must read OPEN (>1 MΩ). If still showing ~10 kΩ, one resistor is still in place — find and remove it.

Why disable: bus pull-up duty transfers to TMP102's 4.7 kΩ (power-bay node) + PCA9685's 10 kΩ (wand node) per the reconciliation in EXTERNAL_SENSOR_INTEGRATION_PLAN.md §2.

5.3 Optional: ALERT line solder

The ALERT pin (J11) on the INA226 is a separate single pin not on the 4-pin logic header. Wire ONLY if the hardware OC/UV latch is used (rare). If so, solder a tail to that pin and route to a free Pi GPIO. The committed GPIO map is tight — pick a genuinely unused header pin before soldering.

6.1 Solder the 5-pin JST-XH PCB-mount male header

Technique: through-hole header solder (§4.1).

7. Part 3: TMP102 (Wand) — Address Mod (0x48 → 0x49) + Header Solder

Generic TMP102 boards (2026-10-23): the §7.1 trace-cut + bridge mod is SparkFun-specific. On a generic board whose ADD0 is a header pin, set 0x49 by wiring ADD0 to 3V3 in the cable instead, never left floating. The wand epoxy bond also needs a flat back face (Power_Bay §16b.3).

Goal: modify the SparkFun Qwiic TMP102's ADD0 jumper to relocate the I²C address from factory 0x48 to 0x49 (so it doesn't collide with the bay TMP102 on the shared bus), then solder a 4-pin JST-XH PCB-mount male header for cable wiring to the rail 30's TMP 4-pin (col 13).

7.1 ADD0 mod — trace cut + V+ bridge (technique §4.5)

The SparkFun board ships with ADDR tied to GND via the ADD0 jumper → factory address 0x48. To move to 0x49 we cut that GND-tie AND solder-bridge ADDR to V+. TMP102's ADDR pin has no internal pull-up, so cutting the GND tie alone leaves ADDR floating → undefined.

Location (as-photographed, current board rev): ADDR jumper cluster on the back, below the vertical "ADDR" silk. Two 3-pad rows sharing the center ADDR pads: lower row = 0x49 | ADDR | 0x48 (the 0x48 side ships factory-bridged — that's the ADDR→GND tie to cut; the 0x49 side is the ADDR→V+ pair to bridge), upper row = 0x4A | ADDR | 0x4B (SDA/SCL ties — leave open). ⚠️ The back view's header pin order is mirrored vs the front: ALERT·SCL·SDA·3V3·GND left→right — beep against the correct pin. (Silk varies between revisions; if your unit differs, identify by continuity — factory state, ADDR reads continuous to GND; that's the tie to cut.)

Cut procedure (technique §4.5):

  1. Loupe-inspect the back of the board (Drdefi 10× magnifier). Identify the ADD0 jumper (the trace bridging the ADDR center pad to the GND rail-tie pad).
  2. Continuity-verify before cutting: multimeter at the IC's ADDR pin (or center pad of the ADD0 jumper) → known GND on the header → should beep.
  3. Cut the GND trace. Sharp X-Acto #11 (fresh — copper dulls quickly). Score the trace 3–5 light strokes until copper is visibly severed.
  4. Continuity-verify after cutting: ADDR pin → GND → should read OPEN (no beep). If still beeps, re-score.

Bridge procedure (technique §4.4):

  1. Fine iron (Weller or Yanecty fine tip) at 350 °C.
  2. Flow a small solder blob bridging the ADDR center pad to the V+ rail-tie pad of the address-select cluster.
  3. Inspect under the magnifier — smooth blob, no bleed to adjacent SDA/SCL rail-tie pads (those would give 0x4A/0x4B, not 0x49).

Continuity truth table (probe the ADDR center pad against the labeled header pins — the beep MOVES from GND to 3V3 as the mod progresses; that migration IS the success signal):

State ADDR ↔︎ GND header pin ADDR ↔︎ 3V3 header pin Address
Factory (0x48 bridge intact) BEEP silent 0x48
After CUT, before bridge silent (OPEN) silent ⚠️ floating — undefined, don't stop here
After V+ BRIDGE (done) silent BEEP 0x49

(Note: in the final state a meter on the resistance range may still show a finite few-kΩ reading ADDR↔︎GND — that's the 3V3 rail's LED/divider paths, not a failed cut. The pass/fail test is the continuity BEEP, not "OL".)

Bench-verify before bonding to heatsink: plug the wand TMP102 into the G469 temporarily via a 4-pin JST-XH cable. i2cdetect -y 1 must show 0x49. Bay TMP102 (still at 0x48) must still appear. If 0x49 missing → re-check V+ bridge (likely cold joint). If 0x48 still on the wand TMP102 → trace cut wasn't complete, re-score.

Safety: before bridging ADDR to V+, confirm the bay TMP102 is also wired and at 0x48 — so when you bench-verify, you can distinguish "wand mod failed" (only 0x48 shows) from "wand mod worked, bay TMP102 disconnected" (only 0x49 shows). Both should appear together once everything is right.

10. Part 6: LED MCPCB — Wire-to-Pad Solder (technique §4.6)

Goal: solder 24 wires total (12 channels × 2 wires per channel) to the 3× LED MCPCB stars (2× RGBW + 1× RGBWW per Electronics_Assembly_Guide.md §12 — cool-biased mix). This is the only "must solder at the LED end" step — LED stars don't have connectors at Ø20 mm.

Substrate: aluminum MCPCB (high thermal mass) — requires elevated tip temp + patient dwell.

10.1 Wire prep

  • 26 AWG silicone-insulated wire (multi-color stock, §10.1 schedule in EXTERNAL doc for color mapping), ~50–90 mm per pair (length depends on driver Z position + LED star angular position — see Lumi_Wand_Integration.md §10.5.5.2).
  • Strip ~3 mm at the LED-end. Tin lightly with a small dab of solder + flux.

10.2 Solder procedure (technique §4.6)

LED MCPCBs are bare metal-core PCB with channel-marked pads (R+/R−, G+/G−, B+/B−, W+/W− — 8 pads total per star, individual-channel layout confirmed 2026-05-31).

  1. Preheat option (recommended): warm the MCPCB on a hot plate at ~100 °C for 30 s before soldering. Reduces iron dwell time on each pad.
  2. Iron: Yanecty, chisel tip, 380–400 °C (yes, higher than usual — the aluminum substrate sucks heat).
  3. Flux: drop of CQ4LF on each pad before soldering.
  4. Tin the pad first — small blob, ~1 s.
  5. Place the tinned wire-end on the pad.
  6. Reflow both together — ~2 s.
  7. Inspect under magnifier: smooth joint with a small fillet. No cold gray dull joint (those are weak — reflow).
  8. Repeat for all 8 pads on each LED star (4 channels × 2 wires) × 3 stars = 24 wires total.

10.3 What "right" looks like

Good MCPCB joint:

  • Smooth shiny dome around the wire
  • Solder visibly wetted into the pad copper (no halo of bare copper at the pad edge)
  • Wire mechanically secured — gentle pull on the wire shouldn't move the joint

Bad MCPCB joint:

  • Cold joint — dull gray surface, looks "frosty." Wire pulls free easily. Reflow with more heat + flux.
  • Insufficient heat — solder blob sits on top of the pad without wetting in. Reflow at 400 °C, longer dwell, fresh flux.
  • Pad lifted — too much heat for too long. Stop soldering on that pad, switch to an adjacent pad if the channel allows; if it's the only pad, the LED star is scrap.

After all 24 wires are soldered, bench-test each star (power up each driver via bench PSU, current-limited to 700 mA per channel) BEFORE moving to the heatsink bonding step in Electronics_Assembly_Guide.md §12.4 — once epoxied, repairs are not possible.

The perfboard carries NO nets. It is a mechanical bulkhead, not a circuit — every conductor runs point-to-point from a jack leg to the far end. No traces, no buses, no jumpers to cut. That is what makes this job tractable on 0.1″ stock, and it is why a pitch mismatch (below) is survivable.

13.1 ⚠️ Pitch check — do this BEFORE you commit a jack to the board

6-pin vertical USB-C legs are not guaranteed to be 2.54 mm pitch. Most are finer (~1.0–1.6 mm) with two large shield/mounting legs outboard. Put one on the 0.1″ perfboard dry and look:

What you see Path
All 6 signal legs drop into 0.1″ holes Path A — through-hole. Standard §4.1 technique, 350 °C, fine chisel.
They don't line up (the common case) Path B — carrier mount. The board becomes purely mechanical: anchor the jack by its shield/mounting legs into whatever holes they do reach (or VHB/epoxy the body down), then solder flying leads directly to the 6 signal legs. Electrically identical — there were never any nets on this board.

Decide per jack, note which path you used, and don't fight a mismatch — Path B is a legitimate outcome, not a failure.

13.2 Solder the jacks (both paths)

Do both jacks first, while the board is bare and flat. Wires last — they get in the way and act as heat sinks.

  1. Shield/mounting legs first. These take 100 % of the plug insertion and removal force; the 6 signal legs must never be the mechanical anchor. Solder them with a medium chisel at 350 °C and be generous — this is a structural joint. Confirm the jack body sits square and its opening is perpendicular to the board face (it has to point radially out through the wall cutout).
  2. Then the 6 signal legs. Fine conical, 340–350 °C, 0.5 mm solder, flux dot first. Minimal solder per leg — the gaps are small and a bridge here is invisible until the port misbehaves.
  3. Dwell discipline: USB-C jacks have plastic between the contacts that deforms and lets pins tilt. ~2 s per leg, then move on. If a leg won't wet, add flux and come back after it cools — don't sit on it.
  4. Inspect under the 10× magnifier, then beep every adjacent leg pair for shorts. Do this now: after the wires are on, access is gone.

13.3 Flying leads

  • Pre-tin the leg and the wire end separately, then join. Wire-to-leg is a butt joint with no hole to hold anything, so both sides must already be wetted.
  • Strip short (~2 mm) and slide heat-shrink on before you solder. Long strips near a fine-pitch row are how neighbours get bridged.
  • Strain-relieve at the board, not at the leg — a wire pulled at the leg peels the pad or bends the pin. Anchor the bundle to the perfboard with a zip tie or a blob of RTV a few mm back, then heat-shrink the whole exit.
  • Shield → GND: tie each jack's shield to that jack's GND lead. Both ends of these runs are the same system; there is no ground-loop case here, and a floating shell just radiates.
  • Do NOT add 5.1 kΩ CC pull-downs. These jacks are passive bulkheads — CC1/CC2 pass straight through to the PD trigger module and to the ESP32 board, each of which does its own CC termination. Adding resistors here presents a second sink and breaks PD negotiation. Six wires, straight through, nothing else.

13.4 ⚠️ Gauge is not the same on both jacks

Covered in full in Lumi_Wand_Flashlight_Variant.md §3.8.3, restated here because it is a soldering-bench decision:

  • Lower (dev) jack — ≤ ~1 A. 26 AWG on all six (palette 2026-09-30; was 28) is fine. Keep D+/D− adjacent and equal length.
  • Upper (charge) jack — VBUS and GND carry the full pack charge current. 28 AWG is rated ~1.4 A chassis and is undersized; use 22 AWG (~7 A) for those two, 28 AWG for CC1/CC2/D+/D−. A 22 AWG conductor on a fine-pitch leg needs a bigger tip and a moment longer — tin it well and keep the joint compact.

13.5 Verify before it goes in the shell

  1. Continuity, every conductor, jack leg → far end.
  2. Adjacent-leg shorts: none, on both jacks.
  3. VBUS → GND on each jack: open (a short here meets a PD brick at 15 V).
  4. Plug a USB-C cable into each jack and tug it. The shield joints hold; nothing moves on the signal legs.

14.3 Wire-to-pad on the buck (FR4 — not the MCPCB regime)

This is ordinary FR4 with a copper pour, not an aluminium-core star. Do not reach for the 380–400 °C of §4.6.

  • Tip: medium chisel · Temp: 350 °C · Solder: 0.8 mm · Flux: dot per pad
  • Procedure: tin the pad (~1–2 s) → tin the wire end → place and reflow together (~2 s). The GND pads sit on a ground pour and pull heat — expect a second or so longer there, and if a GND joint goes dull, add flux and reflow rather than raising the temperature.

14.4 The one gate that matters

Verify 5.00 V ± 0.1 at the buck output — unloaded, then loaded — BEFORE its output wire ever reaches the LCD board (India plan §2.6 step 3). A mis-set or damaged buck presenting 12.8 V to the ESP32 kills it instantly, and it is the only irreversible mistake on this bench.

This procedure exists because the India bench unit shipped bare. The LiLyGO now on order is headered, as is the Waveshare — for either of those you plug in and do not desolder (Flashlight Variant §4.1.1), and none of §15 applies.

The finding below is not contradicted by that. It is about retrofitting a header to a board whose display is already bonded, which is blocked; a factory-headered board has its header fitted before the glass goes on. Both are true.

Bench findings 2026-08-28: the non-touch T-Display-S3 in India shipped with no headers fitted, and — checked on the board — the display glass overhangs the header hole rows on the front face. Both facts together settle the method:

🔴 A header is not possible on this board. Solder wires to the back-side rings.

A through-hole header needs its plastic body seated on the FRONT face (you then solder on the back, where the pins emerge). The glass overhangs those rows, so the body cannot seat. The male headers in the India kit are unusable here — not a supply problem, a geometry one.

Do not try to lift the display to get at the front. It is adhered with its flex tail running to the board; LilyGO's own community carries a "Display removal, T-Display-S3" thread whose recurring outcome is cracked panels. The touch unit is already dead from a suspected flex fault and there is no spare board in India. Four wires are not worth that bet.

This costs you nothing you needed. The whole bench requirement is four conductors, and this is everything they carry:

Wire Pad Feeds
1 3V PCA9685 VCC + TMP102 V+ + the I²C bus level
2 GND common return — breadboard rail, tied to bench-PSU GND
3 GPIO 17 SDA
4 GPIO 18 SCL

The PCA9685 runs on 3.3 V, NOT 5 V — its onboard ~10 kΩ pull-ups go to VCC, so a 5 V supply idles SDA/SCL at 5 V and destroys both the ESP32-S3 GPIOs and the TMP102 (each 3.6 V max). Its V+ screw terminal stays unpowered; LED power comes per-channel from the drivers. See Integration §6.3.

The rig has only two rails: 3.3 V and 12–13.8 V. A fifth wire to the 5V pad (left row) comes later for India §2.6 step 5 — and note that pad is an INPUT, the buck powering the board in place of USB. It is not a rail you distribute to anything. Four joints now, all on the accessible face.

Why no front access is needed: these are plated-through holes — the copper ring exists on both faces — and a wire only has to enter the barrel, not pass through it. Solder wicks down the plating from the back-side ring and forms a proper joint with the front never touched.

(Alternative, if you want something pluggable rather than tethered: press a bare header pin — plastic stripped off — into each hole from the back so it stands proud, and solder it to the back ring. You get 4 pins to plug females onto. Mechanically weaker than a plastic-supported header, since any lateral force goes straight into the pad, so only do this if you'll be re-plugging often. For a rig that gets wired once and left, soldered wire with strain relief is the sturdier answer.)

15.1 ⚠️ Identify the pads by multimeter — the back view is MIRRORED

Every published T-Display-S3 pinout is drawn front-facing. You are working on the back, so pin order runs the other way and counting from the same end will put you one row out. This is the same trap the TMP102 back-side silk sets (§7.1 — "back view = mirrored header order"), and the fleet rule applies: function-ID by meter, never by counting or by silk.

Pad layout, from the vendor pinout (front-facing — remember you are looking at the back):

Row Order, top → bottom
Left (P2) 3V · 1 · 2 · 3 · 10 · 11 · 12 · 13 · NC · NC · GND · 5V
Right (P1) GND · GND · 43 · 44 · 18 · 17 · 21 · 16 · NC · GND · GND · 3V

So 3V sits at the TOP of the left row and the BOTTOM of the right row, 5V at the bottom-left, and there are NC pads mid-row on both sides — which is exactly why counting positions is unreliable and the meter is not optional.

  1. Power the board over USB and let it boot our firmware.
  2. ⚠️ 3V3 reads 0 V unless the board has booted. GPIO 15 (PWR_ON) gates the 3V3 rail feeding both the LCD and the header 3V3 pads; the firmware asserts it in setup() (main.cpp — digitalWrite(PIN_POWER_ON, HIGH), "keep board rails up"). A bare or bootloader-stuck board genuinely measures nothing there.
  3. Find GND first — continuity from a pad to the USB-C shell. There are plenty: two at the top of the right row, two near its bottom, one on the left row.
  4. With the black probe on that GND, sweep the pads for ~3.3 V. There are two 3V3 pads, one at the end of each row. Mark the one you find.
  5. Mark GPIO 17 / 18 by counting from a meter-confirmed pad, not from a board edge — they sit 5th and 6th down the right row, immediately below 43/44. Sanity-check afterwards with the bus scan: wrong pins = no devices found, which is cheap to diagnose and harmless.

Aside — the vendor pinout settles the SDA/SCL question. The same diagram's touch-variant note reads SDA--IO17 SCL--IO18, which is exactly what config.h carries after the 2026-08-27 fix. Third-party pinout pages that list "default I²C = SDA 18 / SCL 17" are the outlier. (On the non-touch board there is no CST816 on those pins anyway, so either assignment works electrically — just wire to match config.h.)

15.2 Solder the wires

Technique: wire into a plated-through hole, soldered on the back-side ring only. Fine chisel, 350 °C, 0.5 mm solder, flux dot per ring.

Per wire:

  1. Prep the wire. Take an M/F dupont jumper, cut the female end off, strip ~3 mm, tin lightly. The surviving male end plugs straight into the breadboard, which is what the Phase 1 table assumes. ~15–20 cm so the board sits clear of the breadboard rather than tethered on top of it.
  2. Tin the back-side ring — flux dot, ~1 s. Small; don't flood it.
  3. Insert from the back, wire entering the barrel far enough to be gripped but flush with the front face — do not push it through. There is a display on the other side.
  4. Fillet to the ring, ~2–3 s. Solder wicks down the plating. Target a small shiny cone around the wire, not a ball sitting on top.
  5. Let it set before you move the wire. Sn60/Pb40 has a 5 °C pasty range (§0) — hold ~2 s after the iron leaves.

Colour convention (matches the fleet wire scheme): orange or red = 3V, black = GND, blue = SDA (17), yellow = SCL (18).

Heat discipline — you are working next to a bonded display:

  • Do the two pairs as separate sessions. 3V/GND are adjacent at one end of the row; 17/18 are adjacent further along. Do one pair, let the board come back to hand-warm, then the other.
  • ~3 s per joint, then off. If a ring won't wet, add flux and come back once it has cooled — never dwell.
  • Never flex the board while a joint is hot. The display bond and its flex tail are the fragile parts, not the rings.
  • Support the board display-up in the helping-hands. The glass must never rest on the bench.

⚠️ Strain relief is not optional here

Because the wire only enters the barrel instead of passing through, the joint is shallower than a normal through-hole joint and a snagged jumper lifts the ring — which on this board is unrepairable in India. Anchor all four wires with a zip tie or a bead of Permatex 81158 a couple of cm back from the joints, so any pull lands on the anchor and never on the copper.

15.3 Verify

  1. No bridges — 10× magnifier over the four joints, then beep each wire against its neighbouring ring.
  2. 3V ↔︎ GND: open. A bridge here shorts the board's own regulator.
  3. Continuity wire-end → ring on all four, and a gentle tug on each (the anchor should take it, not the joint).
  4. Re-boot the board: ~3.3 V at the far end of the 3V wire, and the LCD still runs its boot self-test — that is your proof the display survived the heat.
  5. Only then wire the Phase-1 rig.

Power caution for later: feeding the buck's 5 V into the +5V pad back-feeds the USB path. Do not have USB plugged in while the board is running off the buck (Lumi_Wand_Flashlight_Variant.md §4.1.1 makes the same point about the Waveshare's VSYS).

16.1 Before you touch an iron to a cell

  • Never solder a cell that is hot, swollen, dented, or reading below 2.5 V. Reject it.
  • A slip that bridges + to − is a dead short at tens of amps. Cover every terminal you are not currently working on — kapton, electrical tape, or fish paper. Work one joint at a time.
  • Leave the string un-series as long as possible. Individual cells are 3.3 V; a completed 4S string is 13 V across your workspace.
  • Have the pack tapes and insulating rings cut and ready before you start, so no joint sits exposed while you go looking for materials.
  • Work on a non-conductive surface. Remove watch, rings, bracelet.

16.2 The counter-intuitive rule: hotter iron, less heat in the cell

⚠️ Scope, added 2026-09-06: this rule applies to the STRIP and to the final re-flow — not to raising a cold cell cap from scratch. On the cap's thermal mass a 450 °C tip burns the flux off before the metal reaches wetting temperature, and the joint never takes at all. See §16.3a.

The instinct is to turn the iron down to protect the battery. That is exactly backwards. A cool iron cannot melt the joint quickly, so you hold it there — and total heat into the cell is dwell time × temperature. Dwell dominates.

Iron Serplex at full 80 W
Tip Largest chisel you have. A fine tip has no thermal mass and will stall against a cell cap.
Temperature 450 °C — the hottest operation in this build. Not 500 °C, even though the Serplex goes there — see below.
Target dwell ≤ 3 seconds per joint
Hard stop If it has not wetted in 3 s, STOP. Remove the iron, let the cell return to hand temperature, re-abrade, re-flux, try again. Do not keep pushing.

Why 450 °C and not 500 °C — the limit is the flux, not the iron

The reason to run hot on a cell is not to melt the solder. RA300 is Sn60/Pb40, melting 183–188 °C (§2) — at 350 °C you are already at twice its melting point. The heat is there to punch through the thermal mass of the can: a 26700 is ~90 g of steel and lithium bolted directly to the joint, sinking heat as fast as you deliver it. That is why a modest tip stalls and you end up dwelling, which is the thing that damages cells.

But rosin-activated flux activates around 150–200 °C and begins carbonising above ~300 °C. At a 500 °C tip the flux burns off essentially on contact, leaving carbon that actively blocks wetting. The joint then refuses to take, so you hold the iron there longer — the exact failure the 3-second rule exists to prevent. Above ~450 °C you trade away the flux to buy thermal drive you do not need.

450 °C is the balance: enough drive to beat the can's thermal mass, still inside the window where RA rosin survives the ~2 s it has to work.

Two consequences at 450 °C:

  • ✅ Re-flux every attempt. The first touch consumes it. Never retry onto burnt flux — wipe, re-abrade, fresh dot of CQ4LF.
  • ✅ The tip oxidises faster. Brass wool between joints; hit the tinner if it stops taking solder.

16.3 Why pure nickel fights you (and what to do about it)

99.96 % pure nickel is harder to solder than the nickel-plated steel strip sold for the same job. The material that welds best solders worst — it passivates instantly with a tenacious oxide film. Two consequences:

  • Abrasion is mandatory, not optional. Scuff both the strip and the cell cap to bright metal immediately before fluxing. Solder will not wet an unabraded surface no matter how hot the iron. Use 400-grit silicon carbide wet/dry (Taparia 9″×11″ sheets, in hand 2026-09-05) — SiC cuts nickel better than aluminium-oxide paper and does not load up. Tear off a small square per joint; a fibreglass scratch pen or needle file also works. Abrade dry — no water anywhere near a cell.
  • Use an active rosin flux and use plenty. Never plumbing / acid flux — the residue is corrosive and it is sitting on a battery terminal for the life of the product.

16.3a WHEN IT WILL NOT WET AT ALL — bench finding 2026-09-06

Reported from the bench: cap scuffed with 400-grit emery, fluxed, tried at 400 °C and 450 °C — solder will not take at all. The same iron, same flux, same solder wets the nickel strip with no trouble.

That evidence is the diagnosis: it is THERMAL MASS, not chemistry

Flux, solder and iron are identical between the two surfaces. The only thing that differs is what is behind the metal:

Nickel strip Cell cap
What you are heating 0.15 mm of foil a cap welded to a steel can holding ~100 g of cell
Time to reach wetting temp well under 1 s far longer than the flux survives

The failure mechanism, and why more heat cannot fix it: a 450 °C tip touching a cold, massive cap flashes the rosin to carbon on contact — rosin activates at 150-200 °C and carbonises above ~300 °C (§16.2). The cap is still nowhere near wetting temperature. By the time the mass does come up, the flux is already dead carbon, which actively blocks wetting. So you get nothing — not a poor joint, no joint — and holding longer only pumps heat into the cell.

§16.2's "hotter iron, less heat in the cell" rule is right for the STRIP and wrong for the CAP. On a low-mass part, hot-and-fast wins. On a high-mass part, the flux dies before the metal arrives and the rule inverts.

🛑 First: stop, and treat the cells as suspect. Every failed attempt was heat into a cell with no joint to show for it, and the damage is cumulative and invisible — the cap gasket degrades long before anything looks wrong. Rest the cells 24 h, then re-check each voltage; reject any cell that has drifted, smells, or shows any deformation. Do not keep retrying the same way.

Two cheap diagnostics before anything else:

  1. Abraded vs un-abraded. Fresh flux on a virgin patch of cap, one 3 s attempt. If un-abraded wets better than abraded, you have sanded through the nickel plating to the steel underneath — and rosin flux will not wet bare steel. (The acid flux that would is banned on a battery terminal — corrosive residue sitting on the cell for the life of the product.) Fix: stop using 400-grit; a fibreglass scratch pen or brass brush cleans the oxide without cutting through the plating.
  2. The 60-second window. Nickel re-passivates within about a minute of being abraded — at room temperature, just lying there. If you abrade, then find the flux, then position the strip, then pick up the iron, the surface has already re-oxidised. Abrade → flux within 30 s, everything else laid out ready first.

If you still want to attempt it — three changes, in this order:

  • Flood the flux, do not dot it. A blob gives reserve after the first flash burns off.
  • Drop the tip to ~380-400 °C for the cap only. Slower flux burn-off, and the tip's stored mass does the work rather than its setpoint.
  • Wet the tip with molten solder before contact so heat crosses through a liquid solder bridge instead of dry metal-to-metal. This is the single biggest improvement in transfer rate into a massive part — and use the largest, blunt-faced tip you own for contact area, not a chisel edge.

16.4 Procedure per joint

  1. ⚠️ STRAP THE PACK FIRST — the four cells must be bundled into their final 2×2 geometry and dry-fitted into the shell before a single joint is made (Flashlight §3.6.1.3a). The radial margin is 0.6 mm (62.8 mm bundle in a Ø64.0 mm bore); a pack soldered loose sets oversized and the links are permanent by the time you find out. No rubber bands near the joints at 400 °C — kapton, fibreglass tape or zip ties.
  2. Cut the strip to length measured off the strapped bundle, per Flashlight §3.6.1.3. ~160 mm total needed against 1000 mm supplied — cut generously and practise on offcuts first.
  3. Pre-tin the strip AWAY from the cell. Abrade, flux, tin both ends on the bench. Do as much of the work as possible with no battery involved — this is the single biggest thing that keeps heat out of the cell.
  4. Practise on an offcut until you can wet pure nickel inside 3 s. Do not learn this on a cell.
  5. Abrade the cell cap, scuff to bright metal, flux it.
  6. Tin the cell cap — one quick dab, ≤2 s, then off. Let it cool.
  7. Join: place the pre-tinned strip on the pre-tinned cap, press with the flat of the chisel tip, 1–2 s, remove. Both surfaces are already tinned, so this is a re-flow, not a fresh wet.
  8. Let the cell return to hand temperature before the next joint on the same cell. Do not work a hot cell.
  9. Clean the flux residue off the terminal.

The Lisolec ships with its sampling harness attached. Use the supplied wires — extend in 26 AWG only if they will not reach, and splice away from the pack.

Do the sense joints on the strap BEFORE the strap touches a cell

B1, B2 and B3 land on straps, not on cells. Solder each sense wire to its nickel strap while the strap is still loose on the bench — zero heat into any cell, and far easier to hold. Then fit the strap to the cells per §16.4 as one joint instead of two.

Put a tab on pack+ and pack− too

C1 + and C4 − each have to carry an 18 AWG lead and a 26 AWG sense wire. Landing both directly on the cap is two thermal events on the same cell cap.

✅ Cut a short nickel tab for each of those two terminals, solder the heavy lead and the sense wire onto the tab on the bench, then make one joint to the cell. Halves the heat into C1 and C4 — the two cells that would otherwise take the most.

Technique per gauge

18 AWG onto nickel — the hardest joint in the pack. Both sides are high thermal mass. Pre-tin the wire and pre-tin the tab separately, then join tinned-to-tinned. Stay at 450 °C with the largest chisel. If it stalls, the wire was not tinned properly — do not solve it with dwell.

Why 18 AWG (operator 2026-11-05): at the measured 2.9 A pack peak, 18 AWG runs 3.5 A/mm² (chassis wiring accepts 4–8) and drops about 6 mV over 100 mm, ~9 mV at the 60 W build's ~4.4 A. It is the palette's power gauge and the size an XT30 solder cup takes comfortably; 16 AWG is the cup's limit.

26 AWG onto nickel. The wire is trivial; the strap is the mass. Tin the wire at 350 °C (it needs nothing more and the insulation shrinks back fast at 450 °C), tin the landing spot on the strap at 450 °C, then join briefly. ⚠️ Watch insulation creep — 26 AWG silicone retreats quickly. Strip short, ~3 mm.

Strain relief — both gauges need it, for opposite reasons

  • 18 AWG is heavy enough that its own weight works the joint. Anchor it to the pack within ~20 mm of the terminal.
  • 26 AWG on a strap is fragile in fatigue and will crack at the solder edge under vibration. Tack it down close to the joint.

A dab of RTV or a kapton strap over each does both. Do it before the pack goes in the shell, not after.

16.5a Two techniques that save a heat cycle each

  • ✅ Solder the BMS sampling wires to the NICKEL STRAP, not to the cell terminal. Same electrical node, one fewer thermal event on the cell. See above — do it while the strap is still off the pack.
  • ✅ Heatsink the strip. A crocodile clip on the strap a few mm from the joint pulls heat sideways instead of down into the can.

16.6 Acceptance

  • Visual: shiny, filleted, wetted flat to both surfaces. A dull grey ball sitting proud is a cold joint that has not wetted — the strip is bonded by nothing but solder surface tension and it will fail. Re-abrade and redo.
  • Tug test every joint, firmly.
  • Measure each cell tap after every joint — see Flashlight §3.6.1.4. A cell that has dropped voltage during soldering has been damaged.
  • Let the finished pack rest an hour, then re-measure all four cells. A cell that sags after soldering is a cell to replace, and you want to know before it is inside a sealed wand.

16.7 If it will not take

The fallback in Flashlight §3.6.1.2 is soldering directly to the cell caps with heavy wire and no strip. Same rules — abrade, flux, big tip, ≤3 s. It is worse mechanically (a wire concentrates stress on one point where the strip spreads it), so treat it as a last resort rather than a shortcut.

16a. Battery-lead XT30 + inline-fuse joints (carrier builds, added 2026-10-23)

The carrier's battery lead (BATT, Power_Bay §16c row 7): pack XT30 → F1 → INA226-BATT → PSU CH2.

  1. XT30 solder cups: tin the cup and the 18 AWG stripped end separately, then join with the iron on the cup (~380 °C, a few seconds). Heat-shrink over each cup before the housing goes on. Meter polarity end to end before the first mate: the pack's XT30 gender and polarity are recorded at arrival (§16b.1).
  2. F1 holder pigtails (16 AWG) to the 18 AWG run: an inline solder splice (§4 technique) + heat-shrink, staggered. Never tin a wire end that lands in a screw terminal: the INA226 ends get fork lugs.
  3. Unfused stub ≤ 150 mm from the pack terminal to the F1 holder, the factory pigtail included.
